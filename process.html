The deck Design process

Behind the deck / Design case study

A portrait in
twelve fragments.

I wanted to turn the scattered traces of my digital life into something I could hold, shuffle, and reflect on.

Outcome
12 cards, printed backs, handmade box, companion website
My role
Concept, visual design, QR experiments, web design, print and packaging
Tools
Illustrator, Stable Diffusion, QR Code Monster, HTML & CSS

01 / The question

Can data become a mirror?

Different platforms know different parts of me: the music I listen to, the communities I spend time in, and the ways I present myself online. I was interested in how those fragments might come together as a portrait.

Tarot felt like a fitting framework. Both tarot and data depend on interpretation, even when the results seem certain. I used that overlap to make a deck about identity, with an upright and reversed meaning for each card.

02 / From habit to archetype

What “data-driven” means here

I started by listing the kinds of data I regularly create, including playlists, social activity, and technology habits, then mapped them to symbolic traits. This is a personal, interpretive design process; the cards are not generated from a statistical model or connected to live accounts.

03 / The visual system

Making the code part of the card

I chose Art Nouveau-inspired organic lines and decorative borders to sit alongside the technical structure of a QR code. A limited cream-and-black palette keeps the deck consistent.

The difficult part was balancing imagery with scanability. I used Stable Diffusion and QR Code Monster to explore decorative codes, eventually settling on floral patterns. In Illustrator, I converted the patterns into two-color editable shapes and fitted them into a shared card template.

Some illustration assets were hand-drawn; most came from Adobe Stock. The generated QR patterns, illustration assets, and layout each played a different part in the finished design.

04 / Physical to digital

Shuffle. Select. Scan.

Each printed card links to its own reading. The QR code on the box opens the whole collection. That lets the deck keep the physical ritual of choosing a card while revealing another layer online.

I built the companion pages with HTML and CSS and hosted them on GitHub Pages. The card URLs stay consistent so the printed codes continue to point to the same readings.

05 / Making the object

A deck built by hand

I printed the fronts and backs separately on 300gsm cardstock, cut them out, and glued them together. I also drew the box template in Illustrator, allowing room for the deck and adding tabs for assembly. The first version of the box worked, which was a pleasant surprise.

06 / Outcome and reflection

One identity, more than one reading

The finished prototype brings together twelve cards, custom backs, packaging, and a website. During the original making process, I checked that the printed cards scanned. That was a prototype check, rather than a formal study across devices and lighting conditions.

The most useful lesson was that the interaction had to shape the visual design: a beautiful QR pattern still needs to work as a code. The project also gave me a way to question how much a collection of digital habits can really say about a person.

A future version could explore a larger deck or clearer mappings from specific personal-data records to symbols. Personalized decks and augmented reality are possibilities for further exploration, rather than features of this prototype.
